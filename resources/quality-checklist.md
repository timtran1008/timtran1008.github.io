# Get AI to learn your standard

If you keep telling AI to “make it better,” give it a clearer target. Use examples of work you would actually send to build a checklist, then review the next draft against it.

## 1. Start with examples

Choose two or three examples of the same kind of output: emails, project updates, proposals, or reports. Include a strong and a weak example if you have both. Remove information you cannot share with your AI tool.

## 2. Ask AI to find what matters

Paste this with your examples. Replace [task] with the output you are working on.

```text
I will give you examples of [task].
Do not create a new output yet.

Your job is to infer what "good" means in this context.

For each example:
1. Identify what works.
2. Identify what does not work.
3. Infer the hidden quality criteria.
4. Separate surface style from deeper quality.
5. Produce a reusable checklist.

Mark each criterion as:
- MUST HAVE
- GOOD TO HAVE
- STYLE PREFERENCE
- CONTEXT-SPECIFIC
```

## 3. Make the checklist usable

Read each proposed criterion yourself. Delete vague items such as “sounds professional.” Keep criteria that tell you what to check.

For example: “States the decision needed, names its deadline, and marks missing information as [CHECK]” is more useful than “clear and concise.” This is an illustrative standard; choose the one your work needs.

Keep style preferences separate from factual requirements. Save the checklist with your task instructions.

## 4. Review your next draft

Give AI the checklist and your new draft, then paste:

```text
Use the quality checklist we extracted.

Review this new draft against the checklist.

For each criterion:
1. Mark PASS / PARTIAL / FAIL.
2. Quote evidence from the draft.
3. Explain what is missing or weak.
4. Rewrite only the failed parts.

Do not rewrite everything unless needed.
Do not invent missing facts.
Mark missing information as [CHECK].
```

## 5. Keep your judgment in the loop

Read the evidence AI quotes. Verify important claims against the source. Accept useful fixes, reject mistaken ones, and add any requirement AI missed to the checklist.

## Try it on a weekly project update

Take a good update and a weak update you have permission to use. Look for whether each one tells the reader what changed, what is blocked, what decision is needed, and who does what next. Turn the differences into a checklist for your next update.

This example is a practice exercise, not a measured client outcome. The method comes from Tim Tran’s resource library: find the standard in examples, then use it on new work.
